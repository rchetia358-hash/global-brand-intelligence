# The 91% Problem

**How I tested whether H&M's sustainability story is consistent, or actually credible.**

*MSc International Business dissertation, Anglia Ruskin University, awarded Distinction*
*Role: Solo researcher (design, data collection, coding, analysis, writing)*
*Type: Comparative case study, UK vs Germany*
*Timeline: [June 2026- September 2026]*

---

## A brand can say the same thing everywhere and still be wrong everywhere.

Consistency feels like trust. Same message, same tone, same numbers in every market.

But consistency only tells you a company is *coordinated*. It doesn't tell you it's *telling the truth*.

So I asked: **what if the most polished sustainability story in fast fashion is also the hardest one to check?**

---

## The reality: a very loud, very tidy story

H&M is one of the biggest fast fashion groups in the world, with sales in 75+ markets. It also has a Conscious collection (now discontinued), a garment take-back scheme, and a net-zero-by-2040 roadmap.

At the same time, it runs a high-volume, low-price, fast-turnover model. That is the exact thing sustainability asks you to slow down.

This tension has a name in the literature: the **sustainability paradox**. Commercial success and environmental impact move in the same direction.

And regulators had already noticed. The Norwegian Consumer Authority challenged H&M's claims in 2019. The Dutch ACM pushed it to withdraw its "Conscious" labels in 2022. A US class action was dismissed in 2023 because the marketing language was qualified enough.

That last one stuck with me. It suggested H&M's claims can survive scrutiny **when the qualifying language is there**. So the real question became: *is that language used the same way everywhere, or does it depend on who is checking?*

---

## The insight gap (not a competitor analysis)

I didn't list competitors. I looked at what existing research on this topic does, and what it quietly skips.

- Standardisation vs adaptation theory describes whether brands stay consistent or localise. It never asks if the message is *true*.
- Greenwashing research flags misleading claims. It almost always treats them as one global phenomenon.
- Prior scrutiny of H&M concentrated on Norway, the Netherlands and the US. Nobody had compared the UK and Germany, two markets with very different enforcement cultures.

The gap: **nobody was testing consistency and credibility together, against an outside benchmark.**

---

## The first wrong direction

My first idea was clean and, honestly, a bit too convenient.

The UK is regulator-led, civil, price-driven. Germany has NGO litigation, a tougher evidentiary standard and EU rules. So I expected H&M to behave differently in each: bolder in the UK, more cautious in Germany.

I built the whole research proposal around that tension. A brand being pulled between "one global voice" and "local scrutiny." It was a good story.

It was also a hypothesis, and I'd quietly started treating it like a finding.

---

## The turning point: the data refused to cooperate

I coded UK and German content across five themes: materials, climate, circular economy, supply chain, and regulatory communication.

Four of the five came back with **matching evidence scores** in both markets.

Same targets. Same language. Same framing, pulled from the same Group-level framework. I also couldn't find any UK-specific or Germany-specific sustainability reports at all.

The only real difference was circular economy: garment repair and collection services in selected German flagship stores. But that's a handful of stores, not a national strategy. I had to be careful not to oversell it, and I had to stop myself from reading it as the "adaptation" I was hoping for.

So my headline hypothesis mostly collapsed. H&M doesn't tell two different stories. It tells **one**.

That was the moment I stopped asking "where does H&M differ?" and started asking "okay, if it's the same everywhere, is it *good*?"

---

## The key insight

> **Consistent communication is not the same thing as credible communication.**

The conflict wasn't between markets. It was between **what H&M reports** and **what can be independently verified.**

---

## The solution direction: stop reading the claims, start testing them

I needed a way to separate "what H&M says" from "what holds up." So I built the study around three questions:

1. Is the message consistent across markets? (Global brand consistency)
2. Where does it adapt? (Local adaptation markers)
3. Can the claims be checked against someone else's evidence? (Claim credibility)

And I made one rule: **corporate documents are communication artefacts, not proof.** Annual reports tell me how H&M frames things. They don't tell me what's true.

That meant bringing in outside benchmarks: the **Fashion Revolution Transparency Index** (2019 to 2022) and the **Good On You** rating.

---

## Product thinking: the decisions behind the method

**Why qualitative, not quantitative?**
Counting how often "sustainable" appears tells you nothing about how it's being used. I needed to read what's said *and* what's left out.

**Why a critical realist stance?**
Pure positivism ignores framing. Pure constructivism makes it impossible to compare claims to external evidence. Critical realism lets me say: the organisation is real, the communication is strategic, and both can be examined.

**Why three theories, and why that was risky.**
Legitimacy, signalling and brand authenticity each answer a different question:
- Legitimacy: *why* does H&M communicate this?
- Signalling: *how* is it structured, and can it be verified?
- Authenticity: *how* is it received?

The danger is overdetermination: one finding can "fit" all three. So I tied each theory to separate evidence instead of reading all three loosely against the same material.

**Scoring that doesn't pretend to be something it isn't.**
I scored evidence strength (1 = limited or indirect, 2 = explicit and attributable). These are **not** sustainability performance scores. I said that repeatedly, because it would be very easy to misread them.

**The constraints I couldn't fix:**
- One coder, so no inter-rater reliability.
- H&M's data runs to 2025; Fashion Revolution's stops at 2022.
- Mostly self-reported corporate data.
- No production-volume data.

I chose to state these openly rather than hide them. They shape every conclusion.

---

## What the analysis actually showed

**The 91% number.**
H&M's share of recycled or sustainably sourced materials climbed from **57.1% in 2019 to 91% in 2025**. That's real movement.

But when I opened the 2025 report:
- Recycled materials were only **32%**.
- The other **59%** sat in "sustainably sourced," a category partly defined by H&M's own criteria.
- And the figure says nothing about **how much** is being produced.

If volume rises, a higher percentage can still mean more total impact. That pattern is consistent with what de Freitas Netto et al. call a **hidden trade-off**. I was careful: it's consistent with that mechanism, but I found no evidence of intent to deceive.

**The external view.**
Fashion Revolution scores went 61.2% → 72.8% → 67.5% → 65.9% (2019 to 2022). So H&M's own material number kept climbing while external transparency scores fell after 2020.

That doesn't prove H&M got worse. The two datasets measure different things (material composition vs disclosure transparency), and the index criteria may have simply got tougher. But it **is** a credibility tension.

Good On You rated H&M **"It's a start,"** scoring 3/5 on Planet, People and Animals.

---

## Outcome

**The answer to my research question:**
- H&M's sustainability communication is **largely standardised** across the UK and Germany.
- Local difference exists only in **how selected services are implemented**, not in the strategy.
- H&M's claims are **partially substantiated**, but its credibility is **not yet complete.**

The contribution I'm proudest of is a distinction: **communication can be standardised while implementation adapts.** The two operate at different organisational levels, so "consistent" doesn't mean "uniform in practice."

**Recommendations I made to H&M:**
1. Report recycled and sustainably sourced materials separately.
2. Publish material percentages alongside production volumes and absolute impact.
3. Disclose the criteria and certification schemes behind "sustainably sourced."
4. Clearly separate Group commitments, national messaging, and store-level pilots.
5. Explain why internal progress and external scores diverge, instead of leaving it unaddressed.

**Result:** Awarded **Distinction**.

---

## Reflection

**What I got wrong at the start:** I fell for my own hypothesis. The finding I expected (UK/Germany divergence) was more exciting than the one I got (they're basically the same). The better insight came from letting the evidence disagree with me.

**What I'd do differently:**
- Add a second coder to test reliability.
- Bring in independently verified operational data, not just corporate disclosures.
- Include more markets, and non-European ones.
- Track production volume and absolute emissions over time.

**What still feels unresolved:** I can say the 91% figure is *incomplete*, but I can't say whether H&M's total environmental footprint went up or down. That needs data the company doesn't publish in a comparable way, which is arguably part of the point.

**What I took from it:** Whether a message is consistent and whether it's credible are two separate questions, and treating them as one is how a polished story gets a free pass. That applies to brands, products, and dashboards alike.
